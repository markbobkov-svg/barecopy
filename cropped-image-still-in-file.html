A cropped picture still contains everything you cropped

You pasted a screenshot into a report and cropped it down to hide the column next to it. On screen that column is gone. Inside the file it is still there — complete, at full resolution, one drag of a handle away.

What cropping actually does

When you crop a picture in Word, Excel or PowerPoint, Office does not touch the picture. It stores the original image in the file exactly as you inserted it, and writes four numbers next to it saying how much to trim off each side when drawing it. Those four numbers are the crop.

The consequence is simple: the recipient gets the whole picture. They do not need a special tool or any technical knowledge. They click the picture, choose Crop, drag a handle outwards, and whatever you cropped away reappears. OpenDocument files work the same way, with the crop recorded on the picture's style instead.

This is one of the most common accidental disclosures in office documents, because the person doing the cropping is usually cropping precisely to hide something — a salary column, a name, another client's logo, the rest of a dashboard. The cropping feels like deletion. It isn't.

How to check a picture you've already cropped

Open the document, click the picture, and on the Picture Format tab choose Crop. Drag a handle outward. If more of the image appears, that part is stored in the file and travels with it.

Worth knowing: Word's Document Inspector does not report this. It checks document properties, comments, hidden text and similar, and says nothing about pictures that carry more than they show — so a file can pass the inspector and still hand over the cropped-away half.

How to remove the cropped areas for good

  1. Select the picture in Word, Excel or PowerPoint.
  2. On the Picture Format tab, click Compress Pictures.
  3. Tick Delete cropped areas of pictures.
  4. Choose whether it applies to this picture only or to every picture in the file, then click OK.
  5. Save. The cropped-away pixels are gone from the saved copy.

In LibreOffice the equivalent is right-clicking the picture and choosing Compress. Either way the picture is re-encoded, so it is worth doing this on the copy you are about to send rather than on the master you keep editing.

What Barecopy will and won't do here

Detection is free and covers Word, Excel, PowerPoint and OpenDocument files. Removing the hidden parts is a Pro option: Barecopy re-saves each cropped picture as exactly the visible rectangle and drops the crop, so the document looks identical while the rest of the image is gone.

Two cases it deliberately leaves alone, and says so rather than reporting success. A picture used by more than one shape is skipped, because cropping it once would crop it everywhere it appears. And pictures stored as EMF, WMF or TIFF are skipped, because a browser cannot re-encode those formats — for those, use the Compress Pictures route above.

Frequently asked

Does cropping delete the cropped part?

No. It only changes how much of the picture is drawn. The whole original stays in the file, and dragging the crop handles back out reveals it. Deleting it takes Compress Pictures with "Delete cropped areas of pictures" ticked.

Does saving as PDF get rid of it?

Usually yes for the crop itself — a PDF export draws only the visible part. But it is not a guarantee across every exporter and version, and a PDF brings its own hidden content, from earlier revisions to annotations. Check the export rather than assume.

Why doesn't the Document Inspector catch this?

It was built to check document properties, comments, hidden text, ink and similar — not what pictures carry beyond their visible area. A file can pass the inspector with the cropped-away half fully intact.
